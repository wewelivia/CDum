The finance ministry expects debt to reach 119% of GDP this year, more than 20 percentage points higher than in 2017, and a deficit of 5.4% of GDP, against 2.6% back then. In 2017 France was on its way out of the EU's excessive deficit procedure, the disciplinary process for countries breaching the 3% rule. Today it is back in it.

Hopefuls will note that after 2017 the pressure on French debt receded quickly. But that relief came because the election removed a binary risk: the candidate promising a referendum on leaving the euro lost. Today's problem is arithmetic, and no election result makes a 5% deficit disappear.

Our base case is that the spread stays wide through the April vote and narrows only if the winner arrives with a credible plan and a majority to pass it. The risk case is a fragmented result, or a winner campaigning on more spending, which pushes the spread wider and drags other euro-area borrowers with it.
