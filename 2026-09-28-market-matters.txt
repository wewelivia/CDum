Market Matters — draft for Monday 28 September 2026
What's moving markets?
Too much of a good thing

Last week we said September's flash business surveys would give an early read on how companies had coped with dearer oil. They coped rather well, and that turned out to be the problem. The US composite index jumped to 58.4 from 56.0 in August, the fastest pace of business activity growth in more than five years, with manufacturing at 57.0 and services at 58.7. Survey compilers also flagged building cost pressure. Bond markets read the combination as inflationary and sold off hard. The 30-year US government bond yield reached 5.50% on Thursday, its highest since 2004, and the 10-year touched 5.22%, a level last seen in 2007. Every point on the US yield curve except the two-year now pays more than 5%. That matters because the Federal Reserve raised its target range to 3.75% to 4.00% on 16 September and 16 of its 18 policymakers expect at least one further rise this year. St Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem said on 21 September that more increases would probably be needed and that acting earlier would be preferable. The next decision comes on 28 October.

Missiles, pipelines and a possible deal

Our other question last week was how quickly Saudi exports would recover, with the port of Yanbu then holding only five to seven days of supply. Saudi Aramco restarted the East-West pipeline on 22 September, roughly nine days after drone damage shut it. Relief did not last. Brent settled at $106.60 on Thursday, up 3.4%, after Saudi Arabia intercepted six ballistic missiles fired by Yemen's Houthis towards Taif and the Yanbu area. US crude settled at $94.61, up 2.7%. Both came off session highs, which had been close to 5%, on reports that American and Iranian negotiators meeting in New York had discussed a phased reopening of the Strait of Hormuz in exchange for lifting the US blockade. Brent finished the week near $105, having travelled a range of more than $10 to get there.

Pomp, and not much else

Xi Jinping was received at the White House on Thursday. The two sides extended their trade truce by two months, to 10 January 2027, which keeps current tariff rates in place while negotiators keep talking. There was no agreement on advanced semiconductors and no movement on Taiwan. Markets treated it as the non-event it was. The S&P 500 closed 0.02% lower on the day and Asian shares fell on Friday as investors waited for detail that did not arrive.

What does this mean for investors?
Life above 5%

Last month we flagged the rise in long-dated US yields and set out a cautious view for holders of longer-duration US bonds. That caution has been rewarded, and the question now is whether the level compensates. Higher yields mean lower prices for existing holders, but more income for new money, and a 30-year bond above 5.4% offers a starting yield unavailable for two decades. The same arithmetic works against shares, because a higher risk-free rate raises the bar every other asset must clear. Gold under a real test

Gold fell more than 2% over the week to around $4,271 an ounce, with silver down more still, as surging yields lifted the dollar and raised the cost of holding an asset that pays no income. This is the trade we described earlier this month, when expectations of larger US Treasury buybacks lifted gold and cryptocurrencies before those gains faded. The debasement worry has not gone away, but the bond market is setting the price. [Your view here]

France, and the cost of waiting

We noted last week that France was selling 10-year debt at yields last seen in 2008, with a draft 2027 Budget then expected on 30 September. That presentation has slipped to 1 October, and the backdrop has deteriorated in the meantime. Scope Ratings cut France from AA- to A+ on 18 September, and the finance ministry now projects public debt at 119.3% of GDP this year and 121.7% next. The gap between French and German 10-year yields widened to 112 basis points intraday, a 14-year high, before closing at 109.4. [Your view here]

What are we looking at this week?
The gauge the Fed actually watches

August core PCE inflation, the measure the Fed prefers to the more familiar consumer price index, is published on Wednesday. It is the first reading of underlying price pressure since the Fed raised rates, with the annual rate previously at 3.3%.

Paris shows its hand

France presents its draft 2027 Budget to the Council of Ministers on Thursday, with parliamentary scrutiny expected to begin on 13 October. Investors want to see how much deficit reduction the government will attempt and how much can survive a divided parliament.

The first jobs report since the rise

September US payrolls are published on Friday. August delivered 162,000 jobs against expectations of 55,000, and one published preview looks for around 100,000 this time. Drafted to that expectation and to be refreshed once the actual figure is known.
