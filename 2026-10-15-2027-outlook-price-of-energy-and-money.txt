2026 was full of surprises and the consensus got upset more than once. As we try to map out 2027, we believe it’s important to remain humble and acknowledge that the path forward is highly uncertain. This is because we’re navigating a complex (geo)political landscape and a challenging fiscal backdrop while going through what could be the biggest technological revolution ever. To get 2027 right on the macro front, investors need to call two things: the price of energy and the price of money.

The price of energy will largely decide what central banks do and whether the current hawkish expectations are justified. This, in turn, alongside heavy government borrowing, will dictate what the price of money is. Real rates have moved sharply higher this year and are starting to exert pressure on the key driver behind the resilience of the global economy: the artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure build-out. It’s also extremely relevant for governments worldwide as their borrowing costs have soared. But as always, this is only half of the story. The other half, and the more important one, is to get portfolio positioning right. This is a significant challenge at a time when fiscal policy is replacing monetary policy as the main driver of long-term rates and correlations are shifting.

In the face of this uncertainty, investors have two options: duck and wait or accept the challenge. We’re certainly in the latter camp, at least until the music stops. Investing is inherently risky but so is staying put and increasingly so in a world where inflation is likely to remain elevated. If being and staying invested remains a constant, the way to go about it is changing. Artificial intelligence is a theme that is dominating everything: economic and earnings growth, fixed income and equity markets, and increasingly, politics and geopolitics.

As such, investing today can’t be a debate on AI versus not AI. With AI exposure being a given, the question is rather about how, where, and how much. Going into next year, we believe investors should overweight the AI thematic but should increasingly look beyond the picks and shovels of the AI infrastructure build-out. Indeed, capex is likely to grow at a more moderate pace, constraints on building data centres may delay sales, and expectations surrounding the sector are, in our view, demanding. 2027 should be the year when AI starts to visibly impact adopters’ income statements. It will still be marginal in most cases, but this improved visibility should reassure investors that aggregate revenue growth and margin expansion are still possible. For now, though, profit accretion will likely remain at the top of the value chain.

Similarly, we doubt AI-driven productivity gains will be huge in 2027 but they will be real. This is important because higher productivity (and therefore higher growth) is one way to prevent global indebtedness from getting out of control. But because excessive government debt won’t be solved in the next 12 months, we remain relatively cautious on government bonds and expect more turbulence in the weeks and months ahead. For income-oriented investors who are insensitive to the daily mark-to-market of their bond holdings, the environment is more supportive than it’s been in years. Yet, we would guard against extending duration at this juncture. In our view, the sweet spot continues to be in the belly of the curve and higher up the quality spectrum.

The risk-reward is more challenging on the equity side. Earnings growth expectations are elevated, valuations have come down but aren’t a bargain (especially if earnings disappoint), and the binary nature of the macro landscape suggests that sector rotations will be frequent and potentially violent. We don’t think investors can approach 2027 with a pre-established playbook. For the time being, we favour US equities and XXXXX, but we fully expect our positioning to shift as the year progresses.

In this context, we believe investors’ focus should predominantly be on portfolio construction. Although it’s hard to say when the music will stop (if it ever does), it’s almost inevitable that a few beats will get skipped. As such, particular attention should be paid to both proper diversification and some hedging, when appropriate. While bonds may have been the preferred way to cushion equity downside in the past, this is not entirely true today as recent shocks have been supply- rather than demand-driven. Fixed income should still play its role in a growth scare and therefore retain a place in a well-diversified portfolio. To complement it, we believe investors should consider exposure to commodities and other real assets
