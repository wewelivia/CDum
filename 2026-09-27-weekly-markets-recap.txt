Subject: Top of mind for clients: five handles, the wrong barrel, and no power

Team,

Is 5% the breaking point?

The 10-year touched its highest level since 2007 this week and equities went up anyway. Clients want to know whether there is a level that finally bites. We would stop asking about the level and start asking about the composition. Inflation expectations in the Michigan survey did not move at all this week, so what repriced was the real rate, on strong activity data, hawkish Fed speak and a run of poorly received auctions. That is a very different animal from a credibility problem, and it is why the index absorbed it. The risk we would not wave away is that the absorption is narrow: the same handful of names carrying the index are being repriced upward on demand news faster than the discount rate is rising against them, and the median stock is not. Look at small caps and you can see where the damage has already gone. Our view is that this is a growth-and-supply backup rather than a funding accident, we stay neutral duration, and we think the entry point is getting closer rather than further away.

Is the oil story over?

Crude fell for a second straight week on Hormuz diplomacy, and Trump then rejected Iran's seven-day proposal at the weekend. We would be careful about reading the crude tape as the end of the energy shock, because the shock has moved downstream. European diesel's premium to crude hit a record this week, the widest in data going back to 2011. That is a refining and product-supply problem, and refining capacity cannot be conjured on a political timetable. You can see governments reaching that conclusion in real time: Washington floated a diesel export ban, Rome is convening refiners, Berlin cuts fuel duty from 1 October. Our view is that fading energy on ceasefire headlines is the wrong trade, the persistence sits in the product market rather than the barrel, and we continue to hold energy as a positively carried hedge.

Out of power?

A new risk showed up in the AI trade this week and it deserves to be taken seriously: for the first time, the buildout ran into a hard physical constraint rather than a financing one. Oracle issued a force majeure notice on its New Mexico campus after the gas pipeline serving the site slipped by roughly six months on permitting, and the market briefly priced it as a credit event. It probably is not one. Oracle is not walking away from the project, it is protecting payment timing, and reporting suggests it still carries the power risk itself. The more interesting read is that the binding constraint has moved from chips to electrons and permits, which makes the swing variable a regulatory and local-political one rather than an engineering one, and that is a much slower thing to fix. Meanwhile the demand leg broadened in a way we did not expect, toward inference and agentic workloads and, in Akamai's case, toward CPU rather than GPU capacity. Our view is that we stay constructive on compute demand and increasingly wary of anything whose schedule depends on an interconnection queue.

One to watch: the UK Budget on 28 October, where the Chancellor looks minded to accept a thinner fiscal buffer rather than raise taxes further, at precisely the moment gilt yields are least forgiving of that choice.

Best,
Ju

PART A: THE WEEK IN ONE WORD

Premium.

Everywhere you looked last week, somebody was being asked to pay up for something, and the price of waiting went up.

Start with bonds. The 10-year Treasury hit its highest level since 2007. But look at what actually moved. Consumer inflation expectations in the Michigan survey were unchanged from the flash reading. So this was not a credibility problem. It was investors demanding more compensation for time, for supply and for uncertainty, and the auctions this week told you demand was not there at the old price.

That repricing was not just American. Japan's 10-year reached its highest level since 1996, Australia's since 2011. The one big exception was China, where yields sit near record lows and the gap to Treasuries is the widest we have on record. Two economies, two completely opposite problems.

Then the second premium: fuel. Crude actually fell for a second straight week. But European diesel's premium to crude hit a record. The shock has moved out of the barrel and into the refinery, which is why you had Washington floating an export ban and European governments reaching for fuel subsidies.

And a political premium. France is paying the widest spread over Germany since 2012. In Germany, the Chancellor's party was wiped out of a state parliament for the first time in its history.

So: pay more for time, pay more for fuel, pay more for politics. The one thing investors were still happy to pay up for was AI, and that is the whole story of why the index rose in a week like that.

PART B: SPOTLIGHTS

1. The index is hiding the damage
"Equities shrugged off the bond selloff" is wrong. The Nasdaq made new highs while the Russell 2000 fell on the week and is down roughly 6% this quarter. Stop watching the index and watch the small-cap relative line. Three things are driving that gap: small caps carry floating-rate debt at the short end, where the two-year yield hit its highest in over two years; they have no offsetting AI revenue story; and they are more exposed to domestic freight and fuel costs, which is exactly where the energy shock now sits. Bottom line: if the Fed hikes in October, the index probably keeps working and the median stock does not. (Overlaps with the first item in this week's ExCo email.)

2. This is not a US fiscal story
The comfortable explanation for the yield backup is American: deficits plus hyperscaler bond supply. Then look at Japan, where the 10-year reached its highest since 1996, and Australia, at a 2011 high. Decompose it. A common oil-driven inflation impulse. The Bank of Japan normalising, which removes the last structural anchor of global duration demand. Genuine AI-related corporate supply. And, cutting the other way, China near record-low yields with the opposite problem. Bottom line: a US-only framing understates how much of this is a change in who the marginal buyer of global duration actually is, and it will not reverse on one soft US print.

3. The Fed is leaning on a survey
October pricing moved sharply on the flash PMI. Be careful what that number measures. A PMI is a diffusion index. It counts how many firms report an increase, not how large. In a cost-pass-through shock the price sub-indices inflate mechanically, and activity readings can rise on higher nominal revenue. So check the hard data against it. Core durable goods orders genuinely corroborate strength. Michigan sentiment, at a level associated with recessions, flatly does not. Bottom line: the survey and the household are telling opposite stories, and the Fed has chosen the survey.
